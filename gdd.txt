PART 1 — GAME DESIGN DOCUMENT
1. Game Overview
High Concept
Moving Day is a two-player cooperative physics-based party game in which players operate a small removalist company.
Players take on removal contracts, enter procedurally generated houses, carefully remove furniture and belongings, and load everything into their moving truck.
The challenge comes from deliberately awkward character movement, physics-driven furniture handling, environmental obstacles and the need for constant cooperation.
Players must balance speed and efficiency against the risk of damaging customer belongings.
Between jobs, players return to their company headquarters to purchase upgrades, unlock cosmetics, improve their equipment and accept increasingly difficult contracts.
Tone and Audience
Moving Day is a whimsical couch co-op / lobby game in the vein of PlateUp!: easy to pick up, played together on the same sofa, and funny when it goes wrong.
Presentation, feedback and animation should be playful and full of personality ("pizzazz"): grabbing, lifting, dropping and delivering should all feel satisfying and a little over the top.
Failure should be amusing rather than punishing, so players laugh and immediately want another go.
Design Inspiration
The game takes inspiration from:
- PlateUp! — Progression, business management, character customisation, replayability and the accessible isometric presentation.
- Moving Out — Cooperative furniture handling, slapstick physics and chaotic removalist gameplay.
- Overcooked — Time pressure, communication and increasingly challenging scenarios.
These are reference points rather than templates to reproduce. Moving Day should establish its own identity through its removalist theme, character designs and cooperative physics.
Design Pillars
1. Cooperation: Players should genuinely depend on each other to complete certain tasks.
2. Controlled chaos: Physics should create funny situations without making the game uncontrollable.
3. Replayability: Procedurally assembled properties should make contracts feel different.
4. Personality: Cute animal characters and cosmetic customisation should give players ownership over their characters.
5. Progression: Players should feel that their removalist company is developing over time.
6. Whimsy and pizzazz: The game should feel like a PlateUp!-style couch co-op experience, with juicy, cheerful feedback on every interaction.

2. Visual Direction
Camera and Perspective
- 3D isometric perspective.
- Fixed camera orientation.
- Orthographic projection preferred.
- Elevated camera angle similar to PlateUp!.
- Camera should frame both players and the relevant gameplay area.
- Room walls should support cutaway or visibility effects when obstructing gameplay.
The camera should prioritise gameplay readability over cinematic presentation.
Art Style
Stylised low-poly 3D.
Desired characteristics:
- Chunky geometry.
- Simplified shapes.
- Exaggerated proportions.
- Soft, readable colour palettes.
- Simple materials.
- Distinct object silhouettes.
- Minimal unnecessary surface detail.
The game should look cohesive rather than like a collection of unrelated asset packs.
Environment
Properties should feel like recognisable houses while remaining readable from the isometric perspective.
Initial environments:
- Suburban family home.
- Apartment.
- Luxury mansion.
- Old farmhouse.
- Cluttered hoarder house.
Environment assets should be modular and reusable.

1. Raccoon
2. Bear
3. Fox
4. Rabbit
5. Capybara

All characters should be bipedal and capable of performing the same gameplay actions.
Characters should have:

- Readable silhouettes.
The animals should remain cute and approachable, with exaggerated proportions that suit the game's comedic movement.

Use a shared character skeleton and animation architecture wherever practical.
Species-specific features should be implemented through interchangeable or species-specific meshes attached to the shared rig.
Examples:
- Raccoon mask markings and striped tail.
- Bear ears and rounded muzzle.
- Fox ears and bushy tail.
- Rabbit ears and small tail.
- Capybara muzzle and rounded ears.

4. Movement System
Movement should be intentionally less precise than conventional character controllers.
The objective is to make movement feel physical and slightly awkward while remaining responsive.
Movement Characteristics
- Acceleration rather than instant maximum speed.
- Momentum when moving.
- Gradual braking.
- Turning inertia.
- Reduced control when carrying heavy objects.
- Increased difficulty when changing direction quickly.
- Occasional stumbling from particularly abrupt movements or collisions.
Movement Philosophy
Characters should not feel like they are simply moving across a grid.
Players should learn to anticipate momentum and coordinate their movements.
However, the controls must remain predictable enough that players can improve through practice.
Camera-relative Input
Movement input should be translated relative to the fixed isometric camera orientation.
This prevents directional confusion caused by the elevated camera angle.

5. Furniture and Physics
Furniture handling is the central gameplay mechanic.
Every removable object should have configurable physical and gameplay properties.
Furniture Properties
PropertyDescriptionMassPhysical weight of the objectDimensionsPhysical size and doorway clearanceFragilitySensitivity to damageMonetary valueFinancial penalty if damagedCarry capacityNumber of players requiredGrip positionsLocations where players can hold the objectHandling typeHeavy, fragile, awkward, oversized, etc.
Initial Furniture Types
- Cardboard boxes.
- Sofas.
- Refrigerators.
- Televisions.
- Dining tables.
- Wardrobes.
- Beds.
- Cabinets.
- Chairs.
- Desks.
- Pianos.
- Glass cabinets.
- Lamps.
- Decorative objects.
Start with approximately 15–20 furniture and object types.
Cooperative Carrying
Large furniture should require both players.
Example: carrying a sofa.
1. Both players approach the sofa.
2. Each player interacts to grab an available grip position.
3. The furniture becomes cooperatively constrained between the characters.
4. Both players influence the movement of the object.
5. Differences in direction and speed create rotation and instability.
6. Players must coordinate to navigate doorways and obstacles.
If one player moves too quickly, the furniture should rotate or swing.
If players move in opposing directions, the object should become difficult to control.
Dropping furniture should create consequences.
Players are locked to the grip position they grabbed, so turning corners and passing through doorways is a coordination challenge rather than a free sidestep.
Grabbing should snap the player to the grip with a satisfying, readable animation and feedback.
Cooperative carrying should not feel slow: two players working together should move noticeably faster than one player struggling with the same object.
Solo Carrying
Some items that normally need two players can be lifted by one player, but the grip is unreliable.
- A solo carrier loses grip easily, for example when moving fast, turning sharply, or when the object swings or collides.
- When grip is lost the item falls, with the usual damage consequences, and the player may stumble.
- Teams can also lose grip, though far less easily: violent spinning or players pulling in opposite directions shakes the item loose.
- Solo carrying is a risky shortcut that rewards confidence and punishes carelessness, and it never replaces the benefit of working together.
Physics Implementation
Use Godot 4's 3D physics system.
Recommended components:
- CharacterBody3D for player movement.
- RigidBody3D for furniture.
- CollisionShape3D for collision geometry.
- Controlled spring forces for cooperative carrying.
- Torque and damping for rotational behaviour.
Avoid simply teleporting furniture to a fixed position in front of a player.
The carrying system should preserve physical interactions while providing enough control for enjoyable gameplay.
Use configurable physics parameters per object.
Damage System
Damage should depend on:
- Impact velocity.
- Object fragility.
- Object monetary value.
- Collision type.
- Potentially the surface involved.
Examples:
- Scraping a sofa against a wall may cause minor damage.
- Dropping a television may cause significant damage.
- Colliding a fragile cabinet with a doorway may damage it.
- Low-speed contact should not automatically count as damage.
Damage should be readable to the player through visual feedback, sound effects and scoring.

6. Procedural House Generation
Procedural house generation is a major feature.
The preferred approach is prefab-based procedural generation rather than generating arbitrary geometry.
Modular Environment Components
Rooms
- Living room.
- Kitchen.
- Bedroom.
- Bathroom.
- Garage.
- Dining room.
- Hallway.
Structural components
- Walls.
- Corners.
- Windows.
- Doors.
- Floors.
- Ceiling sections.
Connectors
- Doorways.
- Straight hallways.
- Corner hallways.
- Staircases.
- Garage connections.
Generation Process
1. Select a contract difficulty and property template.
2. Determine the required number of rooms.
3. Generate a connected room graph.
4. Place modular room prefabs.
5. Connect rooms through compatible doorways and hallways.
6. Populate rooms with appropriate furniture.
7. Place environmental obstacles.
8. Define the property exit and moving truck location.
9. Validate navigation and furniture clearance.
Important Generation Constraint
Procedural generation must produce playable houses.
Furniture must be removable.
The system should avoid situations where:
- Furniture cannot fit through a doorway.
- Rooms are disconnected.
- The exit is inaccessible.
- Furniture spawns inside structural geometry.
- The truck cannot be reached.
- Objects are trapped behind obstacles.
Implement a validation and retry mechanism.
Difficulty Scaling
Difficulty can increase through:
- More rooms.
- Larger properties.
- Narrower doorways.
- More furniture.
- Heavier furniture.
- Higher-value fragile objects.
- More environmental obstacles.
- Additional time pressure.

7. Core Gameplay Loop
The primary gameplay loop is:
Company headquarters → Contract selection → Property generation → Removal operation → Truck loading → Results → Upgrades → Next contract.
Company Headquarters
Players can:
- Select their animal characters.
- Customise appearance.
- Select company uniforms.
- Purchase equipment.
- Review company progression.
- Accept new contracts.
Contract Selection
Contracts should communicate:
- Property type.
- Expected furniture.
- Difficulty.
- Estimated duration.
- Potential payment.
- Damage risk.
- Optional objectives.
Removal Operation
Players enter the property and remove the required belongings.
They must:
- Navigate the house.
- Pick up objects.
- Coordinate furniture handling.
- Avoid damage.
- Navigate obstacles.
- Load the truck.
Truck Loading
The moving truck should have limited cargo capacity.
Players must arrange furniture efficiently.
Future mechanics could include:
- Objects falling over during transport.
- Poorly balanced loads.
- Limited cargo space.
- Different truck capacities.
- Loading bonuses for efficient arrangements.
Contract Results
Calculate a final score based on:
CategoryPurposeCompletionPercentage of required items deliveredDamageFinancial damage causedTimeHow quickly the contract was completedEfficiencyOptional objectives and efficient loadingCustomer satisfactionOverall job quality
The final result determines payment and reputation.
Progression
Earned money can be spent on:
- Equipment.
- Moving dollies.
- Protective blankets.
- Carrying straps.
- Better trucks.
- Larger cargo capacity.
- Ramps.
- Company upgrades.
- Cosmetic unlocks.
The game should use a roguelite-inspired progression structure, with increasingly difficult contracts and meaningful purchasing decisions between jobs.
Run Structure: Days, Modifiers and Perks
A run is made up of consecutive days, each day being a contract.
Sticky Objects (implemented)
- From day 3 onwards, some objects in a job are stuck together in pairs, drawn with a pink gum blob.
- One pair from day 3, one more every 5 days after that, up to 3. Pairs are always light enough for a team to move.
- This creates a geometric challenge: players must move connected objects as a combined shape.
Run Modifiers: "Pick One" (implemented)
- Every n days (currently 3, at the hub visit) the host picks one modifier from three (shown side by side on a "Pick One" card), similar to PlateUp!.
- Modifiers are detriments, not rewards: each adds chaos for the rest of that run (butterfingers, slippery soles, glass cargo, heavy lifting, extra gum...).
- They are intended to be an eventual run killer. Seven exist: butterfingers, slippery soles, glass cargo, heavy lifting, gum everywhere (physical chaos) and greedy landlord, cheapskate clients (economic pressure).
- All numbers are in scripts/progression/balance.gd. The run slot's details card and the hub list the ones in play.
Running Costs and Bankruptcy (implemented)
- At the end of each day the company pays running costs: $150 on day 1, +$60 per day (Balance.OVERHEAD_*). The briefing shows them and the hub shows the day's cost.
- If the wallet goes below zero the company is bankrupt: the run ends, its slot is deleted (the profile stays) and a Run Over screen shows the run's stats. This is the run killer that modifiers push the team towards.
Perks (planned, a separate system from modifiers)
- Perks are things the player chooses to bring into a run that aid them.
- They are rewards from levelling up, not a difficulty trade.
- Not designed in detail or implemented yet.
Main Menu and Hub
The main menu and the hub are two separate walkable areas that work alike: players walk around a small room and stand on pads to act.
- Main menu: players join in-world, choose a run slot (new run or continue) and, later, visit the wardrobe.
- Hub: in-run only. The players reach it every N days (GameFlow.HUB_EVERY_N_DAYS, currently 3), not every day, as a respite between days. They spend money there and walk onto the truck pad together to start the next job.
- Saving happens only at the start and end of a day.

Hub Shop
In the hub, players can spend the money they earned on:
- Coffees and energy drinks: temporary stat increases that apply on use.
- Outfit items: these earn more money through a style bonus.
- Rare outfit items may be enchanted and add extra effects.

8. Multiplayer
The game must eventually support both local and online two-player co-op.
Development Order
1. Local co-op.
2. Input abstraction and player identification.
3. Multiplayer authority architecture.
4. Online host/join functionality.
5. Replicated player movement.
6. Replicated furniture physics and interactions.
7. Contract state synchronisation.
Local Co-op
Initial implementation:
- Two players on one computer.
- Separate input mappings.
- Controller support planned.
- Shared camera.
Online Co-op
Use Godot's MultiplayerAPI and ENet as the initial networking approach.
The architecture should support:
- Host and client.
- Stable player identifiers.
- Authoritative contract state.
- Player movement synchronisation.
- Furniture interaction synchronisation.
- Damage and delivery synchronisation.
The server/host should own important gameplay decisions such as:
- Furniture delivery.
- Damage calculations.
- Contract completion.
- Currency and scoring.
Avoid synchronising cosmetic-only information every physics frame.
Online multiplayer is not part of the initial local prototype acceptance criteria, but it is a required project milestone.

PART 4 — MVP DEFINITION
The first playable version should be deliberately small.
Required Features
- Fixed isometric camera.
- Two local players.
- Basic character models.
- Momentum-based movement.
- One small handcrafted test house.
- Five furniture types.
- Single-player and cooperative carrying.
- Basic collision damage.
- Moving truck.
- Delivery tracking.
- Contract results screen.
Explicitly Deferred
- Online multiplayer.
- Full character customisation.
- All six finished animal models.
- Procedural house generation.
- Company headquarters.
- Progression and unlocks.
- Large furniture catalogue.
- Multiple property themes.
These features remain part of the intended game, but should not delay validation of the core gameplay.
MVP Success Criteria
The MVP succeeds if two players can enter a house, work together to remove furniture, experience funny but controllable physics interactions, load the truck and receive a meaningful result.
The most important question is whether moving a sofa through a difficult doorway with another player is enjoyable.
Everything else builds on that.
